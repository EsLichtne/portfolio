Context

About the product

AskBefore is a Berlin MedTech startup building an ecosystem for access to testing for sexually transmitted infections.

It offers users two products: a map where you can find a clinic and book a test, and secure sharing of results between people. The products don’t know about each other — you can use one for a year and never open the other.

Team and my role

The product team had 5 people: a founder with a Senior Product Designer background, a fullstack developer, a mobile developer, a part-time designer and me.

I joined when both products were at the MVP stage. After user testing, I made the exchange clearer and shorter, and made choosing in the aggregator more predictable.

Exchange: explaining an unfamiliar flow

What was wrong

Results exchange is a product people never had before. There’s nothing to compare it to: no familiar entity, no known pattern. In testing, people opened a screen and didn’t understand where they were or what was expected of them.

The aggregator can lean on familiar patterns: a map, a catalog, a cart. The exchange had to explain the mechanics themselves — why you need a folder, who requests the results and what the recipient will see. So all the work here came down to one question: how to make something clear that people have never seen before.

When they conflict, simplicity wins

In testing, participants didn’t understand where they had landed, who was requesting their results and what to do next.

I broke down the feedback: for every line from the interviews, I wrote down what exactly went wrong for the person — on which screen and what they misunderstood. I watched every recording and gathered the sprint materials together with the product screens in one file.

We scored as a team. Five axes described usability, two more described tone: how the product explains safety and how human it sounds. It was a working scale for analysis, not a product metric.

“Simple” and “warm” scored lowest. In decisions they sometimes conflicted: an extra explanation or visual touch supported the person but also demanded their attention.

The principle we chose: where they conflict, simplicity wins. Caring for the user here means, above all, making the path clear. We kept warmth where it doesn’t cost steps: we updated outdated illustrations and added onboarding to every part of the product.

Create the folder first, fill it later

Before, creating a folder took seven steps; now it takes three: create it, come up with a key, save it. After that the folder already exists — you can preview it, show the QR code or come back to it later.

What changed was the moment of creation, not the set of features. Before, a person had to make every decision about the contents first. Now uploading results, requesting them from another person and styling the folder are edits to the folder, and they can be done in any order — they don’t cost steps.

We didn’t remove a single feature. We removed the requirement to go through them all in a row to get a result.

We tested on a working prototype

I built the prototype directly in code, inside the product.

We invited the participant who had struggled the most in the previous round and walked her through the new path. One person isn’t a sample; it’s a worst-case check: if the path doesn’t work for her, there’s nothing more to discuss.

Desktop turned out to be the wrong medium

These runs led to a conclusion that wasn’t part of the task: the exchange had to move to a mobile app.

The first reason is the pattern. Exchanging results is a conversation between two people, and a phone suits that kind of exchange: show and scan a QR code, look at the results.

The second is the ability to restrict screen capture. A person is showing someone their medical documents, and on the web there’s nothing you can do about that. A native app has a mechanism for it. It doesn’t fully solve the problem, but the difference between “impossible” and “inconvenient” matters here.

The app is in development now, led by the second designer. I set the principle for the move and handed over guidelines; she laid out the flows. I run regular reviews and step into the design work in the hard places.

Aggregator: bringing order to choice

The first version of the aggregator already had tests, packages and providers on the map. I picked up the work when new selection criteria appeared. They had to fit in without turning the catalog into one long list of everything available.

The catalog is what to test for, filters are where and on what terms

Filters sat in the same place as tests and packages — one shared selection block under the heading “Filters”. Neither the team nor the user could see the picture: it was unclear what was being chosen and what was refining the choice.

I separated them by what they are. Tests and packages are the catalog: items a person takes. Filters are attributes of items already chosen: not what someone takes, but how one option differs from another.

This set a rule for future changes: a new test goes into the catalog, a new search condition goes into the filters. The split depends on the element’s role, not on where it’s most convenient to put it on the current screen.

Content grew out of what providers actually do

The founder led the negotiations with providers. She brought back data on how their offers differed, and I decided which differences became filters, where they sit and what they’re called in the interface.

For example, one test could be done with different methods, at different prices and turnaround times. That’s how the testing method filter appeared — basic or enhanced testing. Next to it came a price range and date and time selection.

Packages needed separate work on their contents and on how they connect across screens.

The first set appeared as a marketing hypothesis — based on situations in which a person might want to get tested. After a discussion with a clinic, the contents were revised: the provider grouped tests by a different logic.

Meanwhile, the package was described separately on the map, in the cart and in the partner portal. I brought it together into one entity and defined which attributes are needed at each point.

On the front end, I moved the package contents to a shared place that different surfaces used; the developer then connected the backend.

Order: one interface, different conditions

Two independent provider settings

Negotiations with providers brought a constraint: some clinics already manage appointments in their own system and aren’t ready to duplicate slots in ours. So booking through AskBefore became a separate setting — a provider can turn it on or off.

The second axis is payment: through the platform or on site. In the product it’s determined by the provider’s plan and doesn’t depend on the booking setting.

Two axes give four configurations. For each one, the person needs to understand what they’re placing, when they pay and how they’ll find out the appointment time. They don’t need to know the clinic’s internal configuration for that.

One map for every order branch

A configuration only sets the conditions; from there an order can take different paths: the person came, came after their data was deleted, didn’t come, cancelled themselves or got a cancellation from the provider. Without booking there are six such paths, with it there are eight, and each runs in two payment variants. 29 scenarios in total.

I laid them out on one map: provider configurations on one axis, scenarios on the other. Each cell records what the user sees, which emails they get, what the provider does and what state the order is left in. That’s how I checked consistency: does an email promise anything that isn’t in the order, and does the provider know what’s expected of them. I took the screens for the map from production and designed the paths between them from scratch.

One branch end to end: the time is agreed directly

The situation: a provider has our booking turned off. Their offers stay on the map, but you can’t pick a slot through AskBefore. So the interface has to explain how the person will find out the appointment time.

I laid out the scenario for both sides. The provider gets a notification: contact the person, agree on a time, mark the agreement on our side and add the appointment to their own system.

Instead of choosing a slot, the user sees that the clinic will contact them directly. The platform doesn’t receive the agreed time, so it won’t appear in the order. I made this limitation visible in the interface too.

If it matters to the person to pick a date and time right away, they can use the filter on the map. Then offers without platform booking drop out of the results.

Payment changes the set of available actions

Booking determines how the person agrees on a time; the payment method determines which actions with money are available through the platform.

For payment through AskBefore, I added a refund request branch: the user sends a request, and the provider reviews it on their side. The request itself doesn’t mean a refund yet. This configuration has a longer retention period for order data — it may be needed to resolve a dispute.

With payment on site, refunds through AskBefore aren’t available: the platform didn’t take the payment. The person settles payment questions with the clinic directly, and the interface needs to say exactly that.

The consequence sits next to the action

The map produced field hints, banners, buttons, modals and emails. Similar elements could differ in text and behavior: one configuration let you choose a time, another only let you send a request.

I checked them against one rule: if a limitation affects the person’s decision, they have to see it within the flow itself.

One rule for every branch: wherever the product doesn’t know something, doesn’t control it or is about to do something with the person’s money, it says so. In a domain where people are already anxious, silence reads as a trick.

Two products, one entry point

The exchange lives where it doesn’t break

What remained was to show how the two products live side by side. They share one entry point, but what’s available depends on the medium.

In the target setup, desktop and mobile browsers keep the map and orders; for the exchange, people move to the app, where both flows are available.

We decided not to support exchange on the web. That adds a barrier: without installing the app, a person can’t exchange results through AskBefore. We accepted this deliberately — here the medium affects not only convenience but how much control the user has over their medical documents.

The map, meanwhile, works both on the web and in the app. The products stay independent; all they share is the entry point.

Results and retrospective

Results

What I would do differently

I chose the medium after the redesign, not before. We redrew the desktop exchange, built a prototype, ran a user through it — and only that run showed the flow wasn’t a desktop flow at all.

The question “which medium does this flow live on” should have been asked before drawing screens. Some interactions had to be designed again, this time for mobile.
